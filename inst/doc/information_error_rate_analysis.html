Introduction

This vignette demonstrates two complementary approaches for evaluating lineup evidence:

  1. Expected Information Gain (EIG) following Starns et al., which quantifies how much a response reduces uncertainty about guilt.
  2. PPV-range estimation following Fitzgerald, Tredoux, and Juncu, which reports how positive predictive value changes under different corrections for lineup size.

Both methods use the same data format as ROC/CAC analyses: a data frame with target_present, identification, and confidence columns.

1. Expected Information Gain (EIG)

In plain terms

Before running a lineup, you are uncertain whether the suspect is guilty. After the witness responds, you are — usually — less uncertain. EIG measures how much of that uncertainty the procedure removes, on average.

The unit is the bit, which has a concrete meaning: one bit is the uncertainty in a fair coin toss. If you begin thinking guilt is a coin toss and end up certain, you have gained exactly 1 bit. An EIG of 0 means the responses told you nothing; an EIG of 1 (with a 50% prior) would mean every response resolved the case completely.

The virtue of this is that it grades every response, not just identifications. A confident rejection is informative — it makes guilt less likely — and EIG counts that, where a measure built only from suspect identifications cannot.

The arithmetic

Uncertainty is Shannon entropy. For a probability \(p\) of guilt,

\[ H(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p) \log_2 (1-p) \]

which is 1 bit at \(p = 0.5\) and 0 bits at \(p = 0\) or \(p = 1\).

For each response category \(x\) — a decision crossed with a confidence bin — Bayes’ theorem gives a posterior \(P(G \mid x)\), and the information that response carried is the drop in entropy:

\[ \mathrm{IG}(x) = H\bigl(P(G)\bigr) - H\bigl(P(G \mid x)\bigr) \]

EIG is the average of those, weighted by how often each response occurs:

\[ \mathrm{EIG} = \sum_x P(x)\,\Bigl[H\bigl(P(G)\bigr) - H\bigl(P(G \mid x)\bigr)\Bigr] \]

Two consequences follow. Individual responses can have negative information gain — a response that moves belief towards 0.5 increases uncertainty — but the expectation over all responses cannot be negative. And EIG depends on the prior \(P(G)\), which is set by prior_guilt and is a property of the investigation, not of the procedure.

eig_obj <- make_eig(
  lineup_example,
  confidence_bins = c(0, 60, 80, 100),
  show_plot = FALSE
)

eig_obj$eig
#> [1] 0.2011801

Against a prior entropy of 1 bit, that is 20% of the uncertainty removed — informative, but a long way from resolving the question.

2. PPV-range estimation

Positive predictive value is the proportion of suspect identifications that are correct: hits / (hits + false alarms). The difficulty is the denominator. In field data you observe that someone was identified, but not whether a target-absent identification landed on the suspect or on a foil — and only the former is a wrongful identification.

The response is to estimate the innocent-suspect identifications from the observed errors, and the estimate depends on an assumption about lineup size (Fitzgerald, Tredoux & Juncu, 2023):

  • Nominal size. Errors are spread evenly over all \(k\) members, so \(1/k\) of them fall on the innocent suspect. This shows the risk to an innocent suspect if the lineup had been fair (Fitzgerald et al., 2023), and it requires the lineup to be perfectly fair.
  • Effective size. Errors are spread over the members that actually attract choices, estimated from the data. Fewer functioning alternatives means more errors land on the suspect. The correction assumes that the innocent suspect is one of the more plausible members of the lineup.
  • No correction. Every mistaken identification is treated as an innocent-suspect identification. The worst case, and a genuine lower bound.

Reporting all three gives a range rather than a single figure resting on an assumption that cannot be checked. Only the uncorrected figure rests on no assumption about the suspect; the other two are conditional on where the innocent suspect falls among the members.

The PPVs in this vignette also rest on a prior. With base_rate = NULL, the default, PPV is a ratio of counts, so the implicit prior is the study’s own ratio of target-present to target-absent lineups. lineup_example has equal numbers of each, so every PPV below assumes a prior of .5. Supply the base rate that applies to the case at hand through the base_rate argument.

When the three coincide, and why

lineup_example has a designated innocent suspect: its target-absent lineups record whether the identification fell on the suspect or a foil. When that is known, nothing needs estimating, and the corrections have no work to do:

  lineup_example,
  lineup_size = 6,
  show_plots = FALSE

When they diverge

The corrections are important when no innocent suspect is designated — the usual situation with field data. We can create that case by relabelling the target-absent suspect identifications as foil identifications and removing the suspect_pos column, which is exactly the information loss that occurs when the lineup did not nominate a suspect in advance. Both steps are needed: a non-missing suspect_pos tells make_ppv_range() that a suspect was designated, and the design would then stay designated with zero innocent-suspect identifications, giving PPV = 1 under every correction.

no_designation <- lineup_example
ta_suspect <- !no_designation$target_present &
               no_designation$identification == "suspect"
no_designation$identification[ta_suspect] <- "filler"
no_designation$suspect_pos <- NULL

ppv_estimated <- make_ppv_range(
  no_designation,
  lineup_size = 6,
  innocent_suspect = "estimated",  # state the design rather than rely on "auto"
  show_plots = FALSE
)

Interpretation guidance

  • EIG grades every response category, including rejections, and reports how much uncertainty the procedure removes overall. Use it to compare procedures, or to identify which responses carry the evidence.
  • PPV range answers the question a court asks — how often is an identification of this kind correct — and makes visible how much the answer depends on an untestable assumption about lineup size.

The two are complementary: EIG covers all responses and depends on the prior through prior_guilt; PPV concerns identifications only, and is the quantity with direct forensic meaning. PPV depends on the prior as well, through the study’s ratio of target-present to target-absent lineups or the base_rate argument, so a court needs the base rate for the case at hand.

Technical Details

EIG

Computed over response categories formed by crossing decision with confidence bin. For each category the posterior comes from Bayes’ theorem using the observed likelihoods \(P(x \mid G)\) and \(P(x \mid \neg G)\), estimated as response proportions within the target-present and target-absent arms respectively. When no innocent suspect is designated, make_eig_data() first moves \(1/k\) of the target-absent filler identifications into the matching suspect category, so EIG then assumes a fair lineup in the same way as the nominal-size correction. Entropies are in bits (base-2 logarithms). The prior is set by prior_guilt, defaulting to 0.5, at which the prior entropy is exactly 1 bit.

EIG inherits the binning issue discussed in vignette("calibration_decision_analysis"): it is computed over discrete categories, so a category with no observations in one arm gives an unbounded likelihood ratio, and the number of bins is a researcher decision that affects the result.

PPV range

With a designated innocent suspect, innocent-suspect identifications are counted directly and no correction is applied. Otherwise, writing \(e\) for the target-absent error rate within a confidence bin:

\[ \hat{r}_{\text{nominal}} = e/k, \qquad \hat{r}_{\text{effective}} = e/\hat{E}, \qquad \hat{r}_{\text{none}} = e \]

where \(\hat{E}\) is effective size estimated within the bin. PPV then follows as hits / (hits + estimated innocent-suspect identifications).

Assumptions

  1. Target-present and target-absent lineups are independent samples.
  2. The nominal-size correction assumes a perfectly fair lineup. It shows the risk to an innocent suspect if the lineup had been fair, not an estimate of the actual risk. The effective-size correction assumes that the innocent suspect is one of the more plausible members of the lineup.
  3. Effective size is estimated per confidence bin, so bins with few observations give unstable corrections — check the counts before relying on a bin.
  4. Effective size is itself a plug-in estimate with small-sample bias; see the “Small-sample bias” section of ?esize_T. In near-fair lineups the bias is downward, which makes the correction more conservative, but its direction is not guaranteed: with strongly unequal choices the plug-in estimate can be biased upward. Bins with few target-absent choices deserve particular care.
