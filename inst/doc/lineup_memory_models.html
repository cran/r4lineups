What these models are

A witness viewing a simultaneous lineup sees several faces at once and must somehow reduce several impressions to one decision. Signal detection theory says each lineup member generates a memory strength — culprits tend to generate more than innocent members, but the distributions overlap. What it does not say is how the several strengths are combined into a single choice. That is an open theoretical question, and the models fitted here are three answers to it (Wixted, Vul, Mickes & Wilson, 2018).

Independent Observations. The witness attends to the strongest-feeling face and identifies it if that feeling passes a criterion. Each member is evaluated on its own, so the decision depends on the maximum strength and nothing else. This is the model most people have in mind when they picture a witness scanning a lineup.

Ensemble. The witness judges the best face relative to the lineup as a whole — a face must stand out from its neighbours, not merely feel familiar. Formally the decision variable is the maximum strength minus the lineup mean. This predicts something the first model does not: putting a suspect among high-quality foils should make identification harder, because the comparison standard rises.

Integration. The witness pools evidence across the whole lineup, summing strengths rather than picking out a maximum. It is included as a historically important comparator rather than a recommended default; Wixted et al. found it generally inferior.

BEST-Rest is available for exact replication work, but it is a linear rescaling of Ensemble rather than a distinct account. For a lineup of size \(k\),

\[ DV_{\text{Ensemble}} = \frac{k-1}{k}\, DV_{\text{BEST-Rest}} \]

and the criteria rescale by the same factor, so the two produce identical fits. Reporting both as though they were separate model-selection evidence would be double counting.

Why it is important to know which one is right

These are not interchangeable descriptions. They disagree about what makes a lineup fair. Under Independent Observations, foil quality affects how often foils are chosen but not the evidential value of choosing the suspect. Under Ensemble, foil quality changes the standard of comparison and therefore changes what a suspect identification means. Recommendations about lineup construction depend on which account holds.

What this interface does and does not cover

fit_lineup_models() fits the fair simultaneous-lineup case: one procedure or condition per call, with trial-level target_present, identification and ordered numeric confidence.

Not covered, and what each would require:

Not supported What it would need
Designated-suspect target-absent distributions Target-absent suspect choices are currently pooled with target-absent filler choices. Separating them needs the upstream processor to accept a designated innocent suspect, which the audited revision does not expose
Unfair lineups The models assume every foil is a functioning alternative. An unfair lineup has a smaller effective than nominal size, so lineup_size is misspecified. Fitting with effective rather than nominal size is a workaround, not yet validated in this interface; an explicit model of unequal members would be needed to fit an unfair lineup properly
Sequential lineups The decision variable is defined over simultaneously available members. Sequential viewing needs a different model
Reaction-time models Would require a decision process with a time dimension, e.g. a drift-diffusion account
Hierarchical participant/item effects Fitting is by minimum Pearson chi-square on aggregate cell counts, which discards the participant and item structure entirely. Random effects would need a trial-level likelihood

The first two are the most common in practice, since real lineups are rarely perfectly fair. If your lineup has null foils — members nobody ever chooses — see lineup_table() and the effective-size measures in the main vignette before fitting anything here.

Installing the engine

pyWitness is optional and lives in an isolated virtual environment. Neither loading the package nor running its checks installs any Python software. Run this once, interactively — it is slow, and it is deliberately not executed when this vignette is built:

library(r4lineups)
install_pywitness()
check_pywitness_deps(initialize = TRUE)

Preparing data

Data are simulated here at the level of individual foils. foil_bias gives each foil its own mean memory strength, so the foils are not interchangeable — some are plausible alternatives to the suspect and one is chosen only rarely.

For an Independent-Observations model with zero shared variance use shared_variance = "zero", which selects pyWitness’s dedicated class for that exact limit. Shared variance cancels from Ensemble and BEST-Rest and is fixed at zero for them. Unequal signal variance is requested with variance = "unequal".

Can the fit tell the generating rules apart?

An informative check is whether fitting picks out the rule that generated the data. simulate_lineup_data() implements the same three decision rules, so this can be explored directly. One mismatch should be kept in mind: the data below are simulated with unequal foils (foil_bias), while fit_lineup_models() assumes a fair lineup with exchangeable fillers. Every fitted model is therefore somewhat misspecified, even the one matching the generating rule.

simulate_under <- function(rule, seed = 404) {
  d <- as.data.frame(simulate_lineup_data(
    n_tp = 500, n_ta = 500, d_prime = 1.6, c_criterion = 0.9,
    lineup_size = 6, conf_levels = 9, decision_rule = rule,
    foil_bias = c(0.4, 0.2, 0.0, -0.4, -1.0), seed = seed
  ))
  d$confidence <- round(seq(20, 100, length.out = 9))[d$confidence]
  d
}

recovery <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c("max", "ensemble", "integration"), function(rule) {
  f <- fit_lineup_models(simulate_under(rule),
                         models = c("independent", "ensemble", "integration"),
                         lineup_size = 6, confidence_bins = c(0, 60, 80, 100))
  cc <- as.data.frame(f$comparison)
  get <- function(m, col) cc[[col]][cc$model == m]
  data.frame(
    `Generated by`     = rule,
    `Independent chi2` = round(get("independent", "pearson_chisq"), 1),
    `Independent p`    = signif(get("independent", "p_value"), 3),
    `Ensemble chi2`    = round(get("ensemble", "pearson_chisq"), 1),
    `Ensemble p`       = signif(get("ensemble", "p_value"), 3),
    `Integration chi2` = round(get("integration", "pearson_chisq"), 1),
    `Integration p`    = signif(get("integration", "p_value"), 3),
    check.names = FALSE
  )
}))

Read the p-values, not the chi-squares. The models do not have the same degrees of freedom — Independent Observations estimates five parameters against Ensemble’s four, leaving it one fewer residual degree of freedom — so the model with more parameters is favoured by a raw chi-square comparison purely by being more flexible. Ranking the models by chi-square alone is not a comparison the statistic licenses.

A p-value here is the probability of a discrepancy this large if that model were true; it is not the probability that the model is the correct one, and p-values from non-nested models do not rank them on a common scale. With that in mind, two things emerge.

In the MAX and Integration rows the model matching the generating rule fits acceptably and the others are rejected outright.

The Ensemble row does not. Independent Observations and Ensemble return essentially identical p-values, and neither can be rejected. This is not a case of the wrong model winning; it is a case of the two models being indistinguishable on these data.

Independent Observations and Ensemble make very similar predictions for a fair lineup. Separating them requires either much larger samples or a design built to separate them — most obviously one that manipulates foil quality, since it is precisely there that the two accounts disagree: Ensemble makes the comparison standard depend on the other lineup members and Independent Observations does not. simulate_lineup_data() can vary foil quality through foil_bias, so the question is directly explorable.

Wixted et al. reached a similar conclusion, finding both models plausible with a modest overall advantage for Ensemble. Treat a small chi-square difference between these two as uninformative, and note that a single dataset per rule — as here — is an illustration rather than a recovery study. A proper one would simulate many datasets per generating model and report how often each is selected.

Technical Details

How the models are estimated

The audited pyWitness engine estimates parameters by minimising Pearson chi-square between observed and expected cell counts:

\[\chi^2 = \sum_{i} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]

where the cells are decision-by-confidence-bin categories, separately for target-present and target-absent lineups. Parameters are the target distribution mean (targetMean), the confidence criteria (c1, c2, …), and, where applicable, a shared-variance term.

Why AIC and BIC are NA

It is important to make a point of clarification here. loglik_at_estimate is the multinomial log likelihood evaluated at the minimum-Pearson estimate. It is not a maximised likelihood. AIC and BIC are defined in terms of the maximum of the likelihood, so computing them from this quantity would be invalid, and the package returns NA rather than a number that would look usable and be wrong.

Model comparison here therefore rests on the Pearson chi-square and its degrees of freedom, not on information criteria. Comparing models with different numbers of parameters requires attention to df, which the comparison table reports.

What to check before interpreting

  1. converged is TRUE for every model. A non-converged fit’s chi-square is meaningless.
  2. Expected cell counts are not tiny. The chi-square reference distribution is an approximation that degrades when expected counts fall below about 5; inspect fits$expected.
  3. The engine revision matches the audited one — recorded in fits$engine.

Assumptions

  1. The lineup is fair: every foil is a functioning alternative to the suspect. Null foils violate this and make lineup_size misspecified.
  2. Members are viewed simultaneously.
  3. Signal variance is equal across target and lure distributions unless variance = "unequal" is requested.
  4. Witnesses are independent, and each contributes one trial. Aggregation to cell counts discards any participant or item structure.
