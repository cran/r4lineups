Introduction

This vignette demonstrates five statistical methods for analyzing eyewitness identification data in r4lineups:

  1. Calibration Analysis - Assessing confidence-accuracy correspondence
  2. Bayesian Information-Gain Curves - Evaluating diagnostic value of evidence
  3. Expected Utility Analysis - Decision-theoretic evaluation with cost/benefit tradeoffs
  4. Deviation from Perfect Performance (DPP) - ROC-based truncation-robust metric
  5. ANRI - Bias-corrected resolution index with bootstrap inference

These methods complement the traditional lineup fairness measures by providing analyses of identification accuracy, confidence calibration, and decision-making utility.

They answer different questions.

Calibration analysis asks whether a witness’s confidence means what it appears to mean: when witnesses say they are 90% sure, are they right 90% of the time? It yields a calibration curve and three summary statistics, and it is the oldest of the five in eyewitness work (Juslin, Olsson & Winman, 1996).

Bayesian information-gain analysis asks a different question: given a particular identification response, how much should a rational observer revise their belief that the suspect is guilty? This is the framework of Wells, Yang and Smalarz (2015), and it makes explicit something the other methods leave implicit — that the evidential value of an identification depends on the prior probability of guilt, which is a property of the investigation and not of the witness.

Expected utility analysis goes one step further and asks what a policy should be. Given the costs of a wrongful identification and the benefits of a correct one, at what confidence level should an identification be acted upon? Lampinen, Smith and Wells (2019) set out the framework and show that it can disagree with ROC-based conclusions about which procedure is better.

Deviation from perfect performance (DPP) is a discriminability measure, proposed by Smith, Lampinen, Wells, Smalarz and Mackovichova (2019) as an alternative to partial area under the ROC curve. Their argument is that pAUC does not track the diagnostic utility of a lineup, and DPP does.

ANRI is a small-sample correction to the normalized resolution index. NRI (Yaniv, Yates & Smith, 1991) is positively biased when the number of confidence bins is large relative to sample size; ANRI removes that bias so that resolution can be compared across studies and tested formally.

Data Format

All methods in this vignette require a dataframe with at least the following columns:

  • target_present: Logical. TRUE if the guilty suspect is in the lineup
  • identification: Character. “suspect”, “filler”, or “reject”
  • confidence: Numeric. Confidence rating (any scale)

We use the built-in lineup_example dataset throughout. It contains 700 target-present and 700 target-absent lineups of size 6.

Calibration by Condition

The question here is whether calibration differs between two identification procedures. To answer it we need data from two procedures, and lineup_example records only one, so we simulate them.

The comparison is only meaningful if the two conditions genuinely differ. We give Procedure A the stronger memory evidence (d_prime = 1.8 against 1.1), as might arise from better viewing conditions or a better-constructed lineup, and give the two lineups different foil quality as well. 2. Bayesian Information-Gain Analysis

Bayesian analysis evaluates how much an identification response changes our belief about guilt, quantified as information gain (reduction in uncertainty). The framework is that of Wells, Yang and Smalarz (2015).

The arithmetic, briefly

Let \(G\) denote the event that the suspect is guilty and \(x\) a particular identification response — say, a suspect identification made with confidence above 80. Bayes’ theorem gives the posterior probability of guilt as

\[ P(G \mid x) \;=\; \frac{P(x \mid G)\,P(G)}{P(x \mid G)\,P(G) + P(x \mid \neg G)\,\bigl(1 - P(G)\bigr)} \]

The two conditional probabilities are estimated directly from the data: \(P(x \mid G)\) is the proportion of target-present witnesses who gave response \(x\), and \(P(x \mid \neg G)\) the proportion of target-absent witnesses who did. Their ratio is the likelihood ratio,

\[ \mathrm{LR}(x) \;=\; \frac{P(x \mid G)}{P(x \mid \neg G)} \]

and it is the whole of what the identification contributes. An \(\mathrm{LR}\) of 1 means the response is uninformative; larger values point towards guilt, smaller towards innocence. make_bayes_curves() returns these quantities in $likelihoods:

lik <- make_bayes_curves(
  lineup_example,
  response_categories = "confidence",
  confidence_bins = c(0, 60, 80, 100)
)$likelihoods

A high-confidence suspect identification carries a likelihood ratio of roughly 25 here, while a rejection carries one of about 0.3 — that is, it is evidence against guilt. Note also that the likelihood ratio, not the posterior, is the portable quantity: it belongs to the identification procedure, whereas the posterior additionally depends on the prior, which belongs to the investigation.

One practical warning. If no target-absent witness ever produces a given response, the estimated \(P(x \mid \neg G)\) is zero, the likelihood ratio is infinite, and the posterior curve is pinned at 1.0 for every prior. That is almost always a sign of too few observations or too narrow a bin rather than of perfectly diagnostic evidence, and make_bayes_curves() warns when it happens.

# Plot prior-posterior relationships
plot_bayes_prior_posterior(bayes_result)

The plot shows how each response type updates prior belief:

  • Lines above diagonal: Evidence increases guilt belief (diagnostic of guilt)
  • Lines below diagonal: Evidence decreases guilt belief (diagnostic of innocence)
  • Distance from the diagonal: at a given prior, the further a curve lies from the diagonal, the larger the likelihood ratio of that response. Compare curves at the same prior, not by their local slopes.

The prior-posterior curve is due to Wells, Yang and Smalarz (2015), who introduced it along with the base-rate effect-equivalency curve used later in this vignette. The implementation here is written directly from that paper.

On the shape of these curves. They are curved, and that curvature is not an artefact of any smoothing: no loess or spline is fitted anywhere in these plots. For a response with likelihood ratio \(L\), the posterior as a function of the prior \(p\) is \[ q(p) = \frac{Lp}{1 + (L - 1)p}, \qquad q''(p) = \frac{-2L(L - 1)}{[1 + (L - 1)p]^3}. \] The curvature has one sign throughout: the curve is concave (bowed above the diagonal) when \(L > 1\), convex (bowed below it) when \(L < 1\), and the diagonal itself when \(L = 1\). It is not S-shaped. What the display shows is that the same response moves belief most, in absolute terms, at middling priors, and very little when the prior is already near 0 or near 1.

Information Gain

Information gain quantifies uncertainty reduction, measured in bits: the entropy of the prior minus the entropy of the posterior. Where the posterior curve says where belief ends up, information gain says how much uncertainty was removed:

plot_bayes_information_gain(bayes_result)

Interpretation:

  • Positive values: the response moves belief towards certainty (towards 0 or 1), so uncertainty falls.
  • Negative values: the response moves belief towards 50/50, so uncertainty rises. That is not a sign of misleading evidence: a suspect identification given a low prior raises the probability of guilt towards .5, which is the correct update and also the most uncertain state.
  • Where each curve peaks depends on the direction of the evidence. Responses that point to guilt (suspect identifications) gain most at priors above .5, where they push belief towards 1; responses that point to innocence (rejections, filler identifications) gain most at priors below .5.

The curves bend sharply because binary entropy changes fastest near 0 and 1. That shape is a property of the measure, not of these data or of any smoothing.

Confidence-Based Bayesian Analysis

Analyze by confidence level:

bayes_conf <- make_bayes_curves(
  lineup_example,
  response_categories = "confidence",
  confidence_bins = c(0, 60, 80, 100)
)

# Get suspect responses only
suspect_responses <- grep("^suspect_", unique(bayes_conf$curves$response), value = TRUE)
plot_bayes_prior_posterior(bayes_conf, selected_responses = suspect_responses)

Splitting suspect identifications by confidence separates three quite different kinds of evidence. The highest-confidence band sits well above the others across the whole range of priors, which is the Bayesian restatement of the calibration result found earlier: high-confidence identifications from this procedure are worth considerably more than low-confidence ones.

The three curves stay clearly apart and none is pinned to the top of the plot. A curve that is pinned flat at 1.0 signals the zero-cell problem described above, not evidence of unusual strength.

Base-Rate Equivalency of Evidence (BREE)

The base-rate effect-equivalency curve, also from Wells, Yang and Smalarz (2015), answers a question that comes up whenever two procedures are compared and neither dominates: how much better is one than the other, expressed in units anyone can interpret?

The idea is to express the difference as an exchange rate between base rates. Suppose Procedure A, applied to a case with a 20% prior probability of guilt, yields a certain posterior after a suspect identification. We can then ask what prior Procedure B would need in order to reach that same posterior. If B needs a 40% prior to get where A got from 20%, then A is worth as much as a doubling of the base rate — and a doubling of the base rate is something an investigator has an intuition about, in a way that a difference in pAUC is not.

Plotting that correspondence for every prior gives the BREE curve. plot_bree() draws the base-rate shift \(\Delta\) = prior needed by B − prior used by A, against A’s prior. The dashed horizontal line at \(\Delta = 0\) is the null result of no difference; positive \(\Delta\) means B needs the larger prior, so A’s suspect identification is the more diagnostic, and the further the curve lies above zero, the larger A’s advantage. (Plotting B’s prior directly against A’s would put the reference on the diagonal instead, with A’s advantage above it.)

The two procedures are the ones simulated in the Calibration by Condition section: data_proc_a and data_proc_b are data frames of target-present and target-absent identifications with confidence, one row per witness (not lineup vectors). Procedure A has the stronger memory evidence (d′ = 1.8 against 1.1).

bree_result <- make_bree_curve(
  data_proc_a,
  data_proc_b,
  reference_response = "suspect"
)

plot_bree(bree_result)

The shift is positive throughout, from 0.003 to 0.079, so Procedure B always needs a higher prior probability of guilt to reach the posterior that A reaches — which is to say that a suspect identification from Procedure A is worth more. At a prior of 0.2 for A, B needs 0.255. The shift is largest, 0.079, near a prior of 0.46, and shrinks towards both ends of the scale, where neither procedure’s evidence can move belief much.

3. Expected Utility Analysis

The methods so far describe how good a procedure is. Expected utility analysis asks what should be done about it, and it is the framework of Lampinen, Smith and Wells (2019).

The motivating observation is that discriminability measures are silent about consequences. An ROC curve treats a missed identification of a guilty suspect and the identification of an innocent one as two kinds of error, without saying how much worse one is than the other. But everyone agrees they are not equally bad, and once that is admitted, the question of which procedure is preferable cannot be settled without saying by how much. Lampinen et al. show that ROC analysis and expected utility analysis can rank the same two procedures differently — the dissociation their title refers to — so the choice of framework is not a technicality.

Define Utility Matrix

The costs and benefits must be stated explicitly. That is often felt to be the uncomfortable part of the exercise, but the values are being assumed either way; utility analysis only makes them visible and open to argument.

Four outcomes need a value, on any common scale:

utility_matrix <- c(
  tp =  1.0,   # Correct identification of a guilty suspect: a benefit
  fn = -0.5,   # Failing to identify a guilty suspect: a cost
  fp = -2.0,   # Identifying an innocent suspect: the most serious cost
  tn =  0.5    # Correctly declining to identify an innocent suspect: a benefit
)

The relative magnitudes carry the argument. Here a wrongful identification is set at four times the cost of a missed one (-2.0 against -0.5), and twice the absolute value of a correct identification’s benefit. That is a moral and legal judgement, not a statistical one, and it is the natural place for disagreement. Someone who set fp = -10 would be expressing Blackstone’s ratio; the analysis below would then recommend a considerably stricter confidence threshold. Because the choice is this important, report the sensitivity of the conclusion to it, which is what the base-rate section does for the other key assumption.

The plot shows:

  • Expected utility at each confidence threshold
  • Optimal threshold: Maximum utility point
  • Comparison with “all identifications” strategy

Reading it from right to left: a very strict threshold acts on almost nothing, so little is gained and little is risked. Relaxing the threshold admits identifications that are mostly correct, and utility climbs. Past the optimum, each further relaxation admits more wrongful identifications than correct ones, and because those carry four times the weight, utility falls away again. The maximum is the criterion at which the marginal identification is exactly worth acting on.

This is the shape Lampinen et al. (2019) describe. Note that the curve is usually rather flat near its peak, which is reassuring for practice: thresholds somewhat either side of the optimum lose very little, so the recommendation does not depend on locating the maximum precisely.

Compare Procedures

max_utility is a list rather than a single number: it reports the best expected utility together with the criterion that achieves it, and the hit and false-alarm rates at that criterion. Ask for the element you want.

The optimum can be a confidence criterion or the decision to reject every identification. max_utility$strategy says which, so a small helper turns the result into a rule that can be stated:

utility_rule <- function(m) {
  if (m$strategy == "reject_all") "Act on no identification"
  else paste0("Act on confidence >= ", m$criterion)
}

4. Deviation from Perfect Performance (DPP)

DPP quantifies how far an ROC curve deviates from perfect performance, and it is robust to ROC truncation — that is, to witnesses not using the full confidence scale.

It was proposed by Smith, Lampinen, Wells, Smalarz and Mackovichova (2019) as a replacement for partial area under the ROC curve, but it is contested. Lineup ROC curves cannot be traced over their full range: the observed false-alarm rate only extends as far as witnesses’ willingness to choose takes it, and that endpoint differs between procedures. pAUC handles this by integrating over a restricted region, but the choice of region is then itself a problem. Take the region from the procedure with the shorter curve and information from the other is discarded; take a common region and the comparison is made somewhere neither procedure was actually operating. Smith et al. argue that pAUC consequently fails to track which procedure has greater diagnostic utility, and that DPP does.

DPP takes a different approach. Rather than integrating over a region, it is computed at every operating point of the ROC, that is, at every cumulative confidence threshold, and then averaged. At an operating point \(c\) (their Equation 2),

\[DPP(c) = 1 - \text{Culprit}(c) + \text{Innocent}(c),\]

where Culprit(\(c\)) is the culprit (correct) identification rate and Innocent(\(c\)) the innocent-suspect identification rate at that threshold. DPP(\(c\)) is 0 for perfect performance (every culprit identified and no innocent suspect), 1 for any point on the chance diagonal, and 2 for the worst possible performance. When culprits are present in half of all lineups and the two errors are equally costly, DPP(\(c\)) is one minus the expected utility at that threshold, so the average DPP measures the average utility of the procedure across the confidence levels witnesses actually use. Because every procedure is evaluated at its own operating points, curves of different lengths remain comparable.

This is an active disagreement rather than settled ground, and the pAUC vignette takes up the other side of it.

Different costs and base rates

Smith et al.’s Equation 3 weights the two errors:

\[DPP_{\alpha\beta}(c) = \beta[1 - \text{Culprit}(c)] + \alpha(1 - \beta)\text{Innocent}(c),\]

where \(\alpha\) is the cost of identifying an innocent suspect relative to missing the culprit, and \(\beta\) is the base rate of culprit presence. With \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\beta = .5\) this is half of Equation 2. Weighted values no longer range from 0 to 2, so they are comparable only with other values computed with the same weights.

# An innocent-suspect identification three times as costly as a miss,

When to Use DPP

Use DPP when:

  • You have confidence-based ROC data
  • ROC curves are truncated (witnesses may not use full scale)
  • You want a single performance metric comparable across studies
  • You need to compare procedures with different confidence distributions

5. ANRI: Adjusted Normalized Resolution Index

ANRI corrects NRI for small-sample bias, making it more appropriate for hypothesis testing and group comparisons.

Resolution is the third of the properties confidence can have, alongside calibration and over/underconfidence. A witness could be poorly calibrated — systematically over- or underconfident — and yet have excellent resolution, if their confidence still ranked correct identifications above incorrect ones. Resolution is arguably the most important property in practice, since a systematic bias can be corrected for once known, whereas an inability to discriminate cannot.

NRI (Yaniv, Yates & Smith, 1991) measures this, but it is positively biased: some apparent resolution arises from sampling noise alone. Splitting a sample into more confidence bins gives noise more opportunity to look like signal, so the bias grows with the number of bins and shrinks with sample size. Two studies using different numbers of bins therefore cannot have their NRIs compared directly. ANRI estimates and removes that bias.

When to Use ANRI vs NRI

The considerations, rather than rules:

ANRI is the safer default whenever the bias it corrects could be material — modest samples (say a few hundred or fewer), several confidence bins, or any comparison across studies that binned confidence differently. Because the bias grows with the bin-to-sample ratio, it is exactly the studies most likely to be compared informally that are least comparable on raw NRI. Formal tests should use ANRI, since testing a biased estimate against zero tests the wrong thing.

NRI remains reasonable when the correction is negligible — large samples with few bins, where ANRI and NRI will agree closely anyway — or when the point is to compare with older published work that reported NRI. In the analysis above the two differ by very little, which is what a sample of this size should produce.

Reporting both, as the table above does, costs nothing and lets a reader see the size of the correction for themselves.

Example Write-Up

Every number below is computed from the objects created above rather than typed in, so this passage cannot drift out of step with the analysis it describes. The same practice suits a manuscript.

We analysed identification performance using calibration analysis, deviation from perfect performance (DPP), and the adjusted normalized resolution index (ANRI) with bootstrap inference, using 1400 lineups of size 6 (700 target-present, 700 target-absent).

Witnesses were well calibrated overall (C = 0.073), but systematically underconfident (O/U = -0.229): accuracy in the lowest confidence band was substantially higher than stated confidence implied. Average DPP was 0.7 (0 = perfect, 1 = chance), and the operating point of maximum utility was confidence of 20 or above (DPP = 0.533). Resolution, measured by ANRI, was 0.041 (95% CI [0.021, 0.072]), against an uncorrected NRI of 0.045.

Expected utility analysis assumed a base rate of 50% and the cost matrix u(correct suspect ID) = 1, u(missed guilty suspect) = -0.5, u(innocent suspect ID) = -2, u(correct rejection) = 0.5; that is, a wrongful identification was weighted four times as heavily as a missed one. Under those assumptions the rule “act on confidence >= 30” maximised expected utility (EU = 0.288).

Bayesian analysis showed that high-confidence suspect identifications were the most informative response, with a likelihood ratio of 25 (based on 100 guilty-suspect and 4 innocent-suspect identifications in that band).

Note the last parenthesis. The likelihood ratio there rests on very few target-absent observations, and reporting the counts alongside the ratio is what allows a reader to judge how much weight it will bear.
